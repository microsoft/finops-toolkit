### Accessibility terms

In general, use people-first language (refer first to the person, followed by the disability). To ensure clarity and consistency, this should be the default unless you know a specific audience prefers otherwise.

In some cases, however, identity-first language can be used, because some people and communities take pride in recognizing their disability as an integral part of their identity and feel that person-first language is marginalizing. Always make an effort to know the preferences expressed by a person with a disability or a disability community and defer to these.

In all cases, don't use language that has offensive or insensitive connotations, such as *maimed* or *impaired*.

The following table lists examples of people-first language (preferred), identity-first language (acceptable, context-dependent), and offensive or insensitive language (never allowed).

| Preferred (people-first) | Acceptable (identity-first) | Do not use (offensive/insensitive) |
|---|---|---|
| Person who is blind, person with low vision | Blind person | Sight-impaired, vision-impaired |
| Person who is deaf, person with a hearing disability | Deaf person | Hearing-impaired |
| Person with limited mobility, person who has a mobility or physical disability | Physically disabled person, wheelchair user | Crippled, lame, handicapped |
| Is unable to speak, uses sign language, uses synthetic speech | | Dumb, mute, non-verbal |
| Has multiple sclerosis, cerebral palsy, a seizure disorder, or muscular dystrophy | | Affected by, stricken with, suffers from, a victim of, an epileptic |
| Person without a disability | Non-disabled person, able-bodied person | Normal person, healthy person |
| Person with a prosthetic limb, person with a limb difference, person with an amputation | Amputee | Maimed, missing a limb, birth defect |
| Person with a disability | Disabled person | People with handicaps, the handicapped |
| Person with cognitive disabilities, developmental disabilities, learning disabilities, or dyslexia | Learning disabled, dyslexic person | Slow learner, mentally handicapped, differently abled, Special Ed person, stupid, special needs |
| Person with autism | Autistic person, neurodivergent person | Asperger's |
| functional needs (or paraphrase according to the specific disability) | | special needs |

For an overview of Microsoft accessibility policies, see Accessibility guidelines and requirements.

To learn more about writing that conveys respect to all people and promotes equal opportunities, see the Guidelines for Inclusive Language from the Linguistic Society of America.

### Bit and byte terms

In general, spell out *bit* and *byte* terms on the first mention unless:

- Your audience is familiar with the abbreviation.
- You're working on UI text.

In those cases, or after you've spelled out the term on the first mention, it's OK to use the abbreviation.

- Use abbreviations only with numbers in specific measurements.

**Examples** 128 TB measured in terabytes

- Insert a space between the abbreviation and the numeral, or hyphenate if the measurement modifies a noun.

**Examples** 512 gigabytes (GB) of RAM from 1 GB to a maximum of 2 GB 23 MB/day up to 2 TB of physical memory with 8 TB of address space 200 MB of available hard-drive space a 650-MB limit

- In measurements, when the unit of measure isn't abbreviated, use the singular form of the unit of measure when the number is 1. Use the plural form for all other measurements.

**Examples** 0 megabits 0.5 megabits 1 megabit 15 megabits

- Use *of* to add a modifier to a measurement used as a noun.

**Example** The operation requires 200 MB of available hard-drive space.

- Use commas in numbers that have four or more digits, regardless of how the numbers appear in the UI.

**Example** 1,024 MB

### Computer and device terms

In the modern world, customers get things done with whatever device is handy. When you write, focus on what the customer wants to accomplish. If you must write about the device itself, use the most general term that works—usually, that's *device*. Sometimes, it's *computer,* *phone,* or *wearable device*. Occasionally, it's *laptop,* *smartphone,* or *fitness band.*

### Device interaction terms

Use these verbs to talk about using devices and peripheral devices:

  - Use *turn on* and *turn off,* not *power on,* *power off, switch on,* or *switch off.*
  - Use *set up* to describe preparing hardware or software for first use.
  - Use *install* and *uninstall* to refer to adding and removing hardware drivers and apps. 
  - Use *connect* and *disconnect* to
refer to establishing a relationship between devices (direct or wireless) and connecting a device to a network or the internet.

## Accessibility considerations for special characters

Screen readers skip over some symbols and other nonalphabetic characters without reading them or indicating their presence. This situation can cause accessibility problems:

- Users of a document or webpage lack important information.
- Users of a website or web app can't determine the purpose of buttons and other UI elements.

Be aware of this possibility whenever you write content that users will access in electronic form. Consider the following solutions.

### General approaches

Whenever you can, write out the words that you mean instead of using special characters. For example, write "1 through 10" instead of "1–10."

If you must use special characters, use the correct character for the purpose, as outlined in the preceding table. For example, unless you must match what appears in the UI, use a multiplication sign (×) instead of an asterisk (*) or the letter *x* to represent a multiplication operation.

### Content in HTML and related formats: Character entity references

In content written in HTML or a related language (including Markdown), you can improve the accessibility of *some* special characters by inserting a character entity reference instead of the character itself. This type of reference begins with an ampersand (&) and ends with a semicolon (;).

For example, for a copyright symbol, you can enter `&copy;` instead of the symbol in your code. A screen reader that skips over the symbol itself should read out something like "copyright" for the entity reference, giving users information that they would otherwise lack.

However, because of the way screen readers work, the use of entity references for *all* special characters doesn't necessarily yield a better user experience.

### UI elements: `aria-label` attributes

For UI elements, such as for a website or web app, an alternative to character entity references is the `aria-label` attribute. This attribute is part of Web Accessibility Initiative - Accessible Rich Internet Applications (WAI-ARIA). WAI-ARIA is a set of roles and attributes that define ways to make web content and web applications more accessible to people with disabilities.

The `aria-label` attribute is applicable only to:

- Interactive elements, such as buttons, text boxes, and checkboxes.
- Images.
- So-called *landmarks*, such as a page's header, footer, menu, and main content area.

It isn't intended—or widely supported—for static, non-UI textual content.

If the label text of an applicable UI element includes a special character from the preceding table, but you're not using the specified entity reference, add an `aria-label` attribute to the element to improve accessibility. A screen reader will then read out the attribute's value instead of the element's label text.

The value that you provide for the attribute should give the full name of the element, including the name of the special character as it should be read.

### Example

Assume that an app includes buttons for selecting the number of degrees to rotate an image. The label text for these buttons must include a degree symbol (°). Here are the accessible ways to handle this situation:

- Character entity reference in label text: `<button>90&deg;</button>`
- Character in label text, `aria-label` on element: `<button aria-label="90 degrees">90°</button>`

In both cases, a screen reader should correctly read out "ninety degrees."

- When the unit of measure is spelled out, use the singular form when the number is 1. Use the plural form for all other measurements.

- Spell out *by* in dimensions, except for tile sizes, screen resolutions, and paper sizes. For those, use the multiplication sign (×). Use a space before and after the multiplication sign.

**Examples** 10 by 12 ft room 3" by 5" image 4 × 4 tile 8.5" × 11" paper 1280 × 1024

## Numbers and symbols

### 2D, two-dimensional

Spell out on the first mention if your audience may be unfamiliar with the term. On subsequent mentions, use *2D*.

Always hyphenate *two-dimensional*.

### 3D, three-dimensional

Use *3D* most of the time.

Spell out *three-dimensional* on the first mention if your audience may be unfamiliar with the acronym. Always hyphenate *three-dimensional*.

### 8.5" × 11" paper

Use instead of *8.5-by-11-inch, 8.5 × 11-inch, 8 1/2 by 11-inch,* or other ways of referring to the paper size. Use a straight quotation mark (") to signify inches. Use a multiplication sign (×), not the letter *x.*

### @mentions

A feature that enables users to draw someone's attention to a message, comment, or calendar meeting or event by adding the @ symbol followed by the person's name to the body of the message or item.

When this happens, the person is automatically notified that they were mentioned.

Example

@SanjibSahu, could you look at this?
